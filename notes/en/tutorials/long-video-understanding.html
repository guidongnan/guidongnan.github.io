Long Video Understanding ​

Full text (Chinese): 长视频理解Coverage: June 2025 – August 2026, with pre-2025 work included only as background. Scale: 13 chapters (0–12) + 4 appendices, 1546 papers, every one of them broken down individually in Chapters 11–12.

About this English page

The tutorial itself is written in Chinese. This page is its English companion: it states what the tutorial argues, reproduces the measurements that the tutorial treats as its load-bearing evidence, and gives the full chapter structure. Every derivation, table, diagram and per-paper entry lives in the Chinese edition.

Who this is for ​

Anyone who can already make a multimodal model answer questions about a ten-second clip, and now has to make it work on an hour of video — a lecture recording, a surveillance feed, a full surgical procedure, a day of egocentric footage from smart glasses. The tutorial assumes you know what a ViT and an LLM are, and nothing else about video.

It is deliberately not a survey. It is organised around why the obvious approach fails, and it is unusually insistent that most reported progress in this area is not measuring what it claims to measure.

What this tutorial argues ​

Four walls (Chapter 1) ​

Long video understanding is not one problem but four failure modes that arrive together:

  1. The token wall. One hour at 1 FPS is 3600 frames; at ~576 tokens per frame that is ~2.07M visual tokens. Attention is quadratic in sequence length, so this rules out nearly every model before any question is asked.
  2. The evidence-sparsity wall. The answer usually lives in a handful of seconds. Everything else is not merely useless — it actively dilutes the evidence and drives the model back onto its language prior.
  3. The evaluation wall. The one the tutorial pushes hardest. Benchmarks routinely reward behaviour that has nothing to do with watching the video.
  4. The no-rewind wall. In streaming settings you cannot look back, so state must be bounded and written before you know what will be asked.

Ten families of methods, one decision tree (Chapter 7) ​

Long-context extension, visual-token compression, frame/clip selection, memory, retrieval augmentation (Video-RAG), agentic tool use, RL and post-training, streaming, architectural changes (SSM / linear attention / decode acceleration), and modality extension (subtitles, audio, on-screen text, space). Chapter 7.0 frames all ten as the same object — an evidence-acquisition operator feeding a reader — and gives a cost/benefit table plus an explicit "when not to use this" section for every family.

Evaluation traps (Chapter 5.4) ​

Ten shortcuts that must be ruled out before a number means anything: language-prior leakage, single-frame solvability, losing to uniform sampling, subtitle/ASR dominance, invariance to temporal shuffling, interval-length priors, position bias, uncalibrated judges, data leakage, and single-backbone validation. Chapter 5.3.1 specifies a mandatory calibration procedure for GPT-as-judge.

The measurements the tutorial leans on ​

Every number below is transcribed verbatim from the cited abstract; none of it was reproduced by the author.

Timestamps are the weak point, and confidence does not warn you. Prompted for timestamps, strong VLMs reach as little as 3.8% R@0.5 on Charades-STA, and 77–80% of their wrong predictions carry low output entropy — the models are confidently wrong, and entropy-based error detection performs below a random classifier. The same work shows the failure lives in the task interface, not in perception: holding weights fixed and replacing timestamp generation with yes/no questioning recovers much of the ability (arXiv:2608.08315).

Shuffling the frames often changes nothing. On existing benchmarks, models score very well even when frames are scrambled — evidence that they were never relying on temporal order (arXiv:2512.08979). This is why the tutorial treats a shuffle ablation as a mandatory, three-line experiment rather than an optional one.

A long context window is not long-video understanding. State-of-the-art long-context VLMs can ingest around 1000 frames and still fail to use that length, losing to distractors inside their own context window (arXiv:2507.02001).

Robustness collapses quietly. Under ordinary perturbations — motion blur, glare, occlusion — frontier video reasoning models drop 15–30 percentage points on embodied benchmarks while remaining unaware that their visual evidence has degraded (arXiv:2606.26904).

The ground truth for temporal grounding is a distribution, not an interval. Re-annotate the same query–video pair and independent annotators mark moments overlapping by less than half on a large fraction of samples — which is exactly the threshold R@0.5 is built on (arXiv:2608.07434).

Breaking a task's hidden assumption produces near-zero, not merely lower, scores. Prior state-of-the-art MLLMs optimised for one-to-one temporal grounding score close to zero when a single query legitimately corresponds to several disjoint segments (arXiv:2606.06294).

Accuracy gains have a latency price that is rarely reported. Neuro-symbolic long-video QA improves accuracy by grounding temporal reasoning in formal verification, at up to 90× the latency of plain VLM prompting (arXiv:2602.23553).

Chapter outline ​

Chapter 0 — How to use this tutorial ​

Target reader, prerequisites, what "long" means here, and the one-sentence spine.

Chapter 1 — Definition, boundaries, and the four walls ​

What is in scope, the four walls above, and a wall-to-antidote table.

Chapter 2 — Principles I: how a video enters a language model ​

The five-stage pipeline — decode/sample, visual encoding, connector, position and time encoding, LLM backbone — and a minimal working video LLM assembled from it.

Chapter 3 — Principles II: sparse evidence, budget, recall ceiling ​

Why frame selection is an upper-bound problem; three levels of redundancy; the query-relevance spectrum as a classification axis more useful than method names; four different meanings of "budget"; bounded state in streaming.

Chapter 4 — Task map ​

Six task classes (QA, grounding, generation, retrieval, interaction, specialised) with a trivial-baseline cheat sheet for each.

Chapter 5 — Benchmarks and metrics, plus the evaluation-traps chapter ​

Classical anchors, the 2025-06 → 2026-08 benchmarks grouped by the dimension they probe, metric pitfalls and substitutes, the ten traps, and a reusable evaluation checklist.

Chapter 6 — Data ​

Three data types across three training stages, why long-video annotation is so expensive, the mainstream corpora, new datasets by purpose, what synthetic data does and does not buy, and six practical rules.

Chapter 7 — Methods (the core chapter) ​

The ten families, each with motivation → core idea → mechanism → representative work → trade-offs → when not to use it.

Chapter 8 — Systems and engineering ​

End-to-end latency breakdown, memory budget tables, parallelism, edge deployment, the multi-user cost model, and a reproducible evaluation environment.

Chapter 9 — Practice ​

Five deployment recipes (offline QA, low-latency QA, streaming assistant, video-library retrieval, leaderboard), a seven-step build guide, ten failure modes, and a submission checklist.

Chapter 10 — Open problems ​

Constructing questions that genuinely require the whole video; theory of bounded state; how cheap "look again" should be; representing time; causality and counterfactuals; multi-video and lifelong understanding; standardising the cost–accuracy Pareto front; event-level audio-visual alignment; mechanistic accounts of hallucination; the understanding–generation loop; and a research-opportunity heat/crowding table.

Chapters 11 and 12 — All 1546 papers, one entry each ​

  • Chapter 11 covers the 642 papers cited in the body, four fields each: problem / method / numbers / what to take away.
  • Chapter 12 covers the 904 papers that appear only in Appendix A, two fields each, grouped into the same 20 themes as the appendix.
  • Numbers in every entry are transcribed verbatim from the arXiv abstract; entries say so explicitly when the abstract gives none.

Appendices ​

A — the full 1456-paper list by theme with a monthly distribution table; B — glossary; C — learning paths; D — open-source resources.

Navigation ​
