Span or fidelity, pick one

August 2026 ~7 min long video · memory · budgets

This is about a dilemma that appears the moment you cap how much of the past a generative model may carry. It is under anonymous review, so there are no names and no numbers here — which matters less than you would think, because the dilemma is structural and you can see it without a single measurement.

The trap in a fixed budget

A model generating a long video has to condition on what it already produced. Keeping all of it is not an option — the cost grows with length, and video frames are expensive in a way text tokens are not. So you fix a budget: this many tokens of past, no more, no matter how long the clip runs.

Now the question everyone asks is how much history to keep, and that is the wrong question, because the budget is already fixed. The real question is what you spend it on. And if you spend it on verbatim tokens — actual slices of the actual past — you are forced into a choice that has no good answer:

  • Keep the most recent frames. Excellent fidelity, and no reach at all. The model has a crisp memory of the last second and no idea what the room looked like a minute ago.
  • Keep a slice of the opening and pin it. Now you have reach, but the slice never updates. Everything the model generates gets pulled back toward how the scene looked at the start, which is why systems built this way tend to snap backwards when they drift.

Written down like that, it is obviously a false dichotomy — but only if you are allowed to store something other than the past itself. The escape is to spend the same budget on a learned summary: when a frame's representation falls out of the recent window, compress it and write it into a bank, rather than either keeping it whole or dropping it.

Coarsen, do not truncate

A bank fills up. What you do when it is full is the whole design.

The naive answer is eviction: when there is no room, drop the oldest. That is just the recent-window policy with extra steps — the reach is bounded by capacity again, and the early history disappears entirely rather than gracefully.

The alternative is to make the bank hierarchical, and on overflow merge the most redundant pair into a coarser level instead of evicting anything. Old history does not vanish; it gets blurrier. The whole span stays represented, at a resolution that decays with age, under a constant total cost.

The consequence worth internalising: with a verbatim policy, giving the system a bigger budget does not buy reach past a point — it saturates around the horizon the model was trained to attend to, and further budget is spent on detail it already had. With a coarsening summary, extra budget keeps converting into span. Same knob, completely different exchange rate, because the two policies are buying different goods.

Two ablations, two completely different failures

The design has two parts that are easy to conflate: the hierarchy that decides how the bank ages, and the learned readout that decides what a compressed entry actually contains. I assumed they were two aspects of one idea until I removed them separately.

Replace the hierarchy with a single flat bin of the same total capacity, and the system can only truncate rather than coarsen. Coverage collapses — the number of distinct moments still represented drops sharply — and with it goes the quality of anything that depends on the far past.

Replace the learned compressor with plain averaging and the opposite happens. The span is completely fine; the bank still reaches all the way back. But motion detail is smoothed away, and the generated video becomes noticeably less dynamic — an average of what happened is a stationary description of it.

I like this pair because of how cleanly it separates the concerns, and because it is a good argument for running ablations you expect to be boring. Had both removals produced "slightly worse", I would have concluded the two parts were redundant and probably simplified the wrong one away.

A property becomes a knob

The result I did not anticipate is about control rather than quality.

Plot any of these systems on the plane of how consistent the scene stays against how much actually moves. Every fixed-policy baseline lands on a single point, and that point is pinned by its mechanism: a system that holds still is not choosing to hold still, it just cannot do anything else. You cannot ask it for more motion, because motion is not a parameter it has.

Varying how the budget is spent traces out a curve across that plane instead of a point. Which means dynamism stops being a property the architecture inherited and becomes something you can set — different operating points from the same system, chosen per shot.

I think that is the more interesting claim than any single score, and it is the kind of claim that is easy to lose in a table of headline numbers. "We are better" invites an argument about the metric. "This axis used to be fixed and now it is a dial" is a different sort of statement.

Being honest about consistency

One thing I want on the record, because it is the sharp edge of this whole area.

The standard way to report coherence is some average similarity between neighbouring frames. That number is maximised — exactly, globally — by a video that never changes. So a system that quietly stops moving posts an excellent consistency score, and a system that executes a real camera move through a real scene posts a worse one, and the table says the first is better.

Which means when I report consistency, the comparison that matters is not against the best number in the column. It is against a rollout that did nothing. If a frozen clip would beat me, the number is not evidence about my system; it is evidence about the metric. I have written about that failure mode at more length elsewhere, and it is the reason I would rather show nine timestamps side by side than a single averaged score.

The claim I would defend, stripped of everything I cannot say yet: with a fixed budget, buy a summary of the whole span rather than a slice of it — and check that whatever you gain in consistency was not simply bought by holding still.
